# Before releasing a Suno-assisted song, trace what went in and what comes out

**Watch the companion video:** https://youtu.be/SVlGeaCmVcs

*Evidence as of 2026-10-13 UTC.*

A song can carry your lyrics, melody, recording and performance even when Suno changes part of what listeners hear. Before uploading material or releasing the result, make a record of your contributions and the output you chose. That record can help you ask precise questions about the applicable terms and release requirements; it cannot, by itself, settle who holds which rights. [2](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2573904/episodes/19867166-is-it-still-your-song)

This run’s new reporting and accounts, published from September 21 through October 13, make that check more concrete. They describe one artist’s workflow, one practitioner’s filing method, an investigation of Suno’s terms and a proposed change to music search. None establishes the legal status or likely reception of your song. [7](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/deezer-to-let-users-block-ai-generated-music-from-search-results/)

## Start with your own contributions

Artist Zemi told Dazed that he makes the beats, toplines and production, sings the songs, then takes them into Suno to change the voice before mixing the result himself. This is Zemi’s account, not independent verification of his files. Still, it shows why “made with Suno” can leave out the details a creator needs to track: what they supplied, what Suno changed and what they selected afterward. [1](https://www.dazeddigital.com/music/article/71134/1/suno-is-the-new-auto-tune-meet-the-artists-using-ai-to-go-viral)

On the *Is It Still Your Song?* podcast, host Josh of JG Beats Lab recommends keeping a folder for every song. His examples include a dated original demo and lyric sheet, with files identified by what the creator wrote, what Suno generated or what was rebuilt as a stem. Treat that as a useful documentation method, not a ruling on ownership. For your own project, keep the inputs and chosen outputs together and label each contribution while you can still reconstruct the process. [2](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2573904/episodes/19867166-is-it-still-your-song)

There is also room to choose a narrower role for the tool. Producer Andrea Olson describes using Suno to explore ideas rather than treating its output as her finished product. That is one producer’s choice, not a rule for everyone. It does suggest a question to answer before release: which parts of the final recording came from your original material, which came from Suno and which did you make afterward? [6](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Nl-dGFi5ebw&t=1289s)

## Check claims about rights at their source

Italy’s Competition Authority announced an investigation into Suno’s Terms of Service on October 6. It says the provisions under review *may* be unfair under Italy’s Consumer Code and questions how broadly a copyright license imposed on consumers is defined, including a provision it describes as a waiver of moral rights under Italian law. An opened investigation is not a finding that the terms are unlawful, and this announcement does not tell you what rights attach to a particular release. [3](https://en.agcm.it/en/media/press-releases/2026/10/CV278)

That makes the practical step a specific one: before upload and again before release, read the applicable primary terms and the requirements of the release service you intend to use. Ask which version applies to your account, inputs and intended use. The supplied evidence contains neither Suno’s primary terms nor a distributor rule for a particular song, so this article cannot answer those questions for you. Your contribution folder gives you a clearer basis for checking them. [3](https://en.agcm.it/en/media/press-releases/2026/10/CV278)

The wider licensing dispute raises a different question. Responding to a Sony and Universal suit, Suno said its v6 model was trained on licensed partner content and community interactions, including creations. Music Business Worldwide reports that the labels’ complaint alleges unlicensed copying of recordings; Suno contests the claims. Those are opposing assertions about training, not a decision about your finished track. [4](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/suno-confirms-v6-model-was-trained-on-creations-from-users-as-it-blasts-latest-sony-and-universal-lawsuit/)

The Los Angeles Times reports that companies participating in AI licensing deals had not disclosed artist payouts, opt-in counts or how much say individual musicians had over use of their work. It also reports concern among musicians and songwriters about who benefits. Watch for disclosed terms and payouts as that picture develops, while keeping those industry questions separate from the terms governing your own upload. [5](https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/business/story/2026-09-21/ai-music-licensing-deals-labels-artists-who-benefits)

## Watch the release path, too

Deezer says it plans a search toggle that would let listeners exclude *fully AI-generated* music, with a global rollout before the end of 2026. The supplied report does not establish that the toggle is live, how Deezer would classify a partly AI-assisted song or whether a specific release would be affected. If Deezer is part of your release plan, watch for its eventual classification guidance and check how your work would be described. [7](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/deezer-to-let-users-block-ai-generated-music-from-search-results/)

The pre-release test is therefore concrete: preserve the dated source material and selected output; identify your contributions and Suno’s; then verify the terms and release requirements that actually apply. Keep watching for the Italian investigation’s outcome, more detail on licensing terms and payouts, and Deezer’s published classification rules. Until those answers exist, your records make your questions sharper without pretending to resolve them.
