# What Suno quality tests can actually tell a songwriter

*Evidence as of 2026-10-14 UTC.*

If you are starting with your own melody or rough recording, “better sound” is only part of the question. You also need to know whether a generated version keeps the musical idea you meant to carry through, and whether the export holds up when you listen closely. Recent tests offer useful ways to check those things. They do not produce a reliable overall score for Suno v6. [02-c7]

The evidence here was published within this run’s date window: WaveDisco’s test on September 20, ytyng’s on September 25, Caleb Porzio’s podcast account on October 5, and two creator videos on October 9. Each looks at a different outcome. Reading them together is more useful than treating any one measurement or reaction as a verdict. [02-c1] [02-c3] [02-c4] [02-c5] [02-c6] [02-c7]

WaveDisco’s author regenerated three songs across four model versions, keeping the lyrics and prompt the same each time. They measured LUFS and True Peak with one analysis pipeline. Those numbers moved, but the author found no consistent direction that would establish v6 as better than v5.5 on either measure. That is a finding about the measured outputs, not a judgment that the songs sounded equally good. [02-c1]

One narrower result may matter when you handle exports. For the two songs with both formats available, WaveDisco reports that WAV and MP3 landed at the same LUFS in six comparisons across the tested v6 models. The author says a loudness gap seen in v5.5 was absent there, while calling the two-song result “an illustration, not a study.” If you use both formats, that observation gives you a reason to compare your own files. It does not promise that your next export will behave the same way. [02-c2]

Ytyng measured something else: spectral balance. In its sample, v6 exports had less energy from 2 to 12 kHz and a lower spectral centroid than the older tracks it compared them with. But the v6 side contained two songs and the earlier side six, made from different prompts. The files also used different codecs: Opus in m4a for v6 and MP3 for the earlier tracks. Ytyng explicitly allows that the songs chosen could explain part or all of the measured difference. The author also cautions that a song’s quality has little to do with its spectrum alone. These measurements raise a listening question; they do not establish that v6 generally sounds duller. [02-c3] [02-c7]

The videos shift attention from measurements to musical direction. In one demonstration, the creator added vocal, instrument, and performance directions in stages and judged a later vocal smoother. The creator also acknowledged a competing explanation: another generation of the song might have changed the result. A listener can inspect that example, but the sequence does not isolate which instruction, if any, caused the perceived improvement. [02-c4]

Producer Andrea Olsen tried to carry a voice memo’s melody into a worship song. She reported that Suno kept the melody and largely followed her directions, while still hearing the result as sounding like AI. Those are two separate judgments about one song: following an idea and producing a sound she would accept. Her example is valuable precisely because success on the first question did not settle the second. [02-c5]

Podcast host Caleb Porzio describes wanting to replay songs made from rough recordings. He also points to audible artifacts in AI covers he encountered. His reaction is a listener’s account, not a controlled comparison, but it captures a familiar possibility: a track can be compelling enough to replay and still contain something you would want to catch before release. [02-c6]

These accounts support a practical sequence for evaluating a candidate track. Compare the exports you intend to use; listen for artifacts; then check the melody, vocal delivery, and other directions against your starting idea. Those checks answer different questions. WaveDisco’s three songs, ytyng’s unmatched tracks, and the creators’ individual listening judgments cannot tell you how often another songwriter’s work will pass them. A matched, blinded listening test across more songs and creators would answer more of that broader question. For now, the useful result is a set of checks to apply to *your* next generation, with release readiness still a decision to make track by track. [02-c7]
