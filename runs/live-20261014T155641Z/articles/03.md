# Before You Upload, Know What You Are Contributing

*Evidence as of 2026-10-14 UTC.*

A song can feel ready to release while important questions remain unanswered: which parts did you make, what did you submit to Suno, and how will a receiving service treat the finished track? The evidence gathered for this piece points to three separate checks. None of them, on its own, clears a release. [03-c5] [03-c6]

## Keep the making of the song visible

On the September 29 episode of *Is It Still Your Song?*, JG Beats Lab’s Josh advises creators to keep a dated original demo, lyric sheet, the settings they used, retained stems, raw vocal takes, and final master together. He also suggests identifying what the creator wrote, what Suno generated, and what was rebuilt from a stem. This is a practitioner’s recordkeeping method; Josh also promotes his services, and his account is not a legal determination. [03-c1]

The method has a practical value even before anyone asks about rights. If you later want to explain a particular track, a folder of its inputs and working parts gives you something more precise than a general statement that you “used AI.” It can help you describe your own process. It cannot, by itself, establish your contractual rights, the copyright status of a generated part, or how another service will classify the master. That distinction is an inference from Josh’s advice and the separate questions raised by Suno’s training account and the Italian authority’s investigation. [03-c5]

## Check the upload decision separately

In a statement reported by *Music Business Worldwide* on September 22, Suno said its v6 model was trained on licensed partner content and community interactions, including creations and preference signals. That is Suno’s account of its training. The supplied statement does not show whether any particular creator’s upload was used. Still, it gives a creator a reason to examine the applicable terms before submitting a demo, lyric, or other material they care about. [03-c2] [03-c5]

There is a live question about those terms, but no established violation in the supplied evidence. On October 6, the Italian Competition Authority announced that it had opened proceedings into whether Suno’s Terms of Service may be unfair under the Italian Consumer Code. The authority also singled out contractual provisions on copyright for attention. An investigation is a question being examined by a regulator, not an adverse finding. The supplied material does not include the applicable primary Suno terms, so it cannot verify what rights a given creator grants under a particular version or jurisdiction. [03-c3] [03-c5]

This is why a contribution log and a terms check do different jobs. The log records what you say you supplied or made. The terms are the place to examine what the service says it may do with submitted material. Neither source in this brief establishes the rights in an individual finished recording. Before uploading, the useful question is: *Which current terms apply to my account and material, and what do they say about that submission?* [03-c5]

## Separate promises from outcomes

The commercial picture also calls for careful attribution. The *Los Angeles Times* reported on September 21 that label AI deals had not disclosed what artists would be paid, how many had opted in, or how much say individual musicians had over use of their work. The paper also reported a newer Sony and Universal lawsuit alleging infringement by Suno’s latest model. Those are reported gaps and litigants’ allegations, respectively; they are not an observed artist payout or a court ruling. A creator weighing a partner’s promised benefit should ask for the relevant terms and evidence of an actual outcome. [03-c4]

## Check the destination before release

A later question concerns discovery. According to *Music Business Worldwide*, Deezer said on October 13 that it planned a search toggle allowing listeners to exclude **fully AI-generated** music, with a global rollout planned before the end of 2026. The supplied report does not establish that the feature is live, explain its final classification rules, or show that a particular Suno-assisted song would fall within them. [03-c6]

That uncertainty matters because artists can use the same tool in different ways. *Dazed* described artists who openly use Suno to modify vocals and compare that use to effects such as reverb or auto-tune. Their description does not tell us how Deezer would classify their tracks. If Deezer is a destination for your work, watch for its actual release guidance and classification practice, then evaluate your own track against what the service publishes. [03-c6]

Before release, keep the materials and decisions behind the song together, read the Suno terms that apply when you upload, and check the receiving platform’s current rules. The next evidence to watch is the outcome of the Italian investigation and the published details of Deezer’s planned filter. Until those are available, questions about an individual song’s rights or classification remain questions, not clearance that a general article can give. [03-c1] [03-c3] [03-c5] [03-c6]
