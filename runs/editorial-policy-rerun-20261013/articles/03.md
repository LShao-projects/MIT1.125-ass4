# Before a Suno-assisted release, trace what you contributed and where the recording will go

*Evidence as of 2026-10-13 UTC. Draft from a pilot run; recheck dates before publication.*

If you are turning your own demo into a Suno-assisted track, the release decision starts before the upload. You need to know what you supplied, what you changed afterward, and what the destination platform will do with the finished recording. A dated folder and a few targeted questions can make that decision more concrete, even though they cannot settle ownership or predict how a platform will classify your song. [03-c1] [03-c8] [03-c9]

## Keep a record of the song you made

Josh, founder of JG Beats Lab, recommends keeping the original demo, lyrics, settings, chosen stems, raw vocal takes and final master together, then marking which parts you wrote and which Suno generated. That is a practitioner’s documentation method, not proof of ownership. It gives you a way to reconstruct your process if you later need to explain a vocal, arrangement or production choice. [03-c1]

The value of that record becomes clearer when the workflow is mixed. In an account reported by *Dazed*, artist Zemi says he makes the beats and top lines, sings the song, uses Suno to change the voice, and mixes the result himself. Those are Zemi’s descriptions; the passage does not independently verify each step. Still, “AI-assisted” alone would tell a listener or distributor little about which part of his track was generated. A process record could tell a much more specific story. [03-c7] [03-c1]

Producer and music educator Andrea Olsen offers another pre-upload example: in her video demonstration, she uploads a voice memo she says is her own and urges creators to examine the terms before uploading songs. Olsen also promotes an IP service and a paid course in the video. Her workflow is useful to inspect, but her legal characterizations should be checked against the applicable primary terms rather than treated as a legal determination. [03-c2]

## Check the upload terms before supplying a demo

Why scrutinize the upload itself? *Music Business Worldwide* reports Suno’s account that its v6 training included licensed partner content and community interactions, including creations and preference signals. That is Suno’s description of training inputs. It does not show whether any particular voice memo or song was used, or establish what rights attach to a particular output. [03-c3]

There is also a current question about the wording of Suno’s terms. The Italian Competition Authority announced on October 6 that it had opened an investigation focused on those terms. Among the issues it raised was whether the license sought from consumers is sufficiently defined. These are concerns under examination by the authority, not findings that Suno’s terms are unlawful or a ruling on an individual creator’s upload. [03-c5]

Together, Josh’s record-keeping method, Suno’s reported training account and the authority’s investigation suggest a practical pre-upload pause: identify what is in your demo, preserve evidence of your contribution, and read the terms that apply when you supply it. The supplied evidence does not include the operative Suno terms, so this article cannot say what a particular upload legally permits or transfers. [03-c9]

## Separate industry promises from your own arrangement

Partnership announcements do not answer every artist’s question about consent or payment. The *Los Angeles Times* reports that participating label deals promise artist choice and payment, while the amounts, the number of artists who opted in, and the extent of individual input had not been disclosed. It also reports uncertainty over whether an opt-out would keep recordings out of training or only out of features presented to listeners. Those are reasons to seek the terms of a specific arrangement, not to assume that a partnership resolves your situation. [03-c4]

The litigation calls for the same care with status. *Music Business Worldwide* reports that a new complaint by Sony and Universal accuses Suno of copying label recordings without a license; Suno calls the claims flawed in fact and law. Those are opposing positions, not a court decision about the complaint or a finding that your Suno-assisted release infringes. [03-c6]

## Check the destination, then watch what changes

A release-ready recording also has a destination. *Music Business Worldwide* reports Deezer’s statement that it plans a search toggle allowing listeners to exclude **fully AI-generated** music, with a global rollout planned before the end of 2026. The supplied report does not establish that the toggle is live, that it applies to every AI-assisted song, or how Deezer would classify yours. If Deezer is part of your release plan, watch for the final rule and ask how a track with your own writing, performance or production would be treated. [03-c8]

Before releasing, you can therefore make three checks: preserve the versions that show your work, inspect the terms governing what you upload, and confirm the current requirements of each distribution destination. Keep an eye on the Italian investigation, the litigation, and Deezer’s eventual rollout, while treating each according to what has actually happened. None of the supplied passages can decide the rights or reach of a specific release for you. [03-c1] [03-c5] [03-c6] [03-c8] [03-c9]
