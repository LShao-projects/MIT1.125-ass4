# Piece 03 video script: Before a Suno-assisted release

*Evidence as of 2026-10-13 UTC. Draft from a pilot run; recheck dates before publication.*

**Target length:** About 4 minutes. **Production note:** Use neutral diagrams and screen text. This is a script for Google Vids, not a record of a completed demo.

## Opening — What would you need to show?

**Narration:** Suppose a song began as your voice memo, passed through Suno, and ended as a track you might release. Before you upload the memo or send out the track, ask two questions: What did I contribute? And what terms or platform rules apply at each step? Those checks follow from practitioner advice, Suno’s reported account of training inputs, and scrutiny of its terms. They cannot settle the legal effect of your particular upload. [03-c9]

**Visual:** A simple path: “Your material → Suno → finished track → destination platform.” Leave space beneath each step for a question mark.

## Keep a record of the work

**Narration:** Josh, founder of JG Beats Lab, recommends a song folder containing the original demo, lyrics, settings, selected stems, raw vocal takes, and final master. He also suggests marking what you wrote and what Suno generated. That is a useful documentation method, not proof of ownership. [03-c1]

Dazed reports artist Zemi’s account of singing and producing material, then using Suno to change his voice and mixing the result himself. That is his description; the passage does not independently verify his process. It shows why “made with Suno” may leave out the very details a creator needs to record. [03-c7]

**Visual:** Creator-made folder illustration with labels for demo, lyrics, settings, stems, takes, and master. Add two columns: “Human contribution” and “Generated contribution.”

## Check before supplying material

**Narration:** In her video, producer and music educator Andrea Olsen says she uploads her own voice memo and urges attention to terms before uploading songs. Her video also promotes an intellectual-property service and a paid course. Treat her legal characterizations as advice to check, not as a reading of the terms that applies to you. [03-c2]

There is a concrete reason to ask how supplied material may be used. Music Business Worldwide reports Suno’s statement that its v6 training included licensed partner content and community interactions, including creations and preference signals. That is Suno’s account of the model’s inputs. It does not show whether any particular file was used. [03-c3]

The Italian Competition Authority says it has opened an investigation into Suno’s terms, including a concern about the scope of the license requested from consumers. That is an issue under examination, not a finding against Suno. We do not have the applicable primary terms in this brief, so the practical step is to inspect the terms that apply when you upload, especially the language about your content and license. [03-c5] [03-c9]

**Visual:** On-screen questions: “What am I uploading?” “Which terms apply today?” “What license do they describe?” Do not show a fabricated terms screen.

## Separate promises, allegations, and outcomes

**Narration:** Licensing headlines also need careful reading. The Los Angeles Times reports that participating label deals promise artist choice and payment, while payout details and the number of artists opting in remain undisclosed. If an arrangement matters to your work, seek its specific terms instead of assuming a partnership answers questions about individual consent or compensation. [03-c4]

Music Business Worldwide also reports that a Sony and Universal complaint accuses Suno of copying recordings without a license. Suno calls those claims flawed. Those are opposing positions in litigation; the supplied material gives us no court decision about that complaint or about your release. [03-c6]

**Visual:** Three plain labels: “Deal promise,” “Complaint and response,” “Decision?” Keep the final label as a question.

## Check the destination, then keep watching

**Narration:** Finally, check where the finished track will go. Music Business Worldwide reports Deezer’s plan for a search toggle covering *fully AI-generated* music, with a rollout expected before the end of 2026. The passage does not establish that the toggle is live, that every AI-assisted track qualifies, or how your track would be classified. If Deezer is a destination, watch for the eventual rule and ask how it treats your release. [03-c8]

A release-ready decision still belongs to the creator. Keep the record of your contributions, read the applicable terms before supplying material, and verify the destination’s current rules. Where the evidence ends, keep the question open. [03-c1] [03-c8] [03-c9]

**Visual:** Return to the opening path. Add a check box under each step: “Record contributions,” “Read applicable terms,” “Check destination rules.”
