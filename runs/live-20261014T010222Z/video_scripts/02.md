# Piece 02 — What Suno quality tests actually measure

*Evidence as of 2026-10-14 UTC.*

**Production format:** Google Vids storyboard. Approximately 4:10 at a conversational 128 words per minute. All music imagery is a labeled hypothetical; no Suno audio or product screenshot is needed.

## Story outline

1. A hypothetical songwriter has a chorus idea and wants to know whether a Suno-assisted version is ready to release. The question gives viewers a reason to compare tests. [02-c7]
2. WaveDisco checks export loudness and peaks. Viewers learn what those measurements can answer. [02-c1]
3. Its small format comparison shows why a useful observation is still limited. [02-c2]
4. Ytyng finds a spectral difference, then identifies song and codec differences that complicate its cause. [02-c3]
5. Two creators test musical direction with their own material. Viewers move from measurements to intention, while keeping the limits of those accounts in view. [02-c4] [02-c5]
6. A listener’s account shows why enjoyment and audible artifacts can coexist. [02-c6]
7. Return to the hypothetical chorus: check the property that matters on the creator’s own material; these sources cannot supply an overall release-readiness verdict. [02-c7]

On those measures, he reported no consistent direction of improvement from v5.5 to the three v6 variants. That tells us what happened in his set of outputs. | 1:20–1:59 | There is a smaller finding inside that test. For two songs with both formats available, Serj reported a WAV-to-MP3 loudness gap in v5.5, and no LUFS difference for the three v6 variants. WAV and MP3 are different file formats; MP3 compresses audio. Serj calls those two songs an illustration, not a study. If export consistency matters to you, compare the files you would actually use. Do not turn this pair of songs into a promise about every export. | Two generic file icons swap places beside a meter. | 1:59–2:42 | What about tone? Ytyng reported less high-frequency energy in its v6 outputs than in older tracks. That sounds like a model comparison, until you see what went into it. The songs and prompts differed, and the v6 and older files used different codecs, the methods used to encode audio. Ytyng says the difference could partly, or entirely, come from the different songs. So a spectral chart can tell our songwriter what those particular files contained. It cannot isolate a general change in the model, and it cannot say whether the chorus feels right. | The tone branch turns into two spectral curves. | 2:42–3:24 | The direction branch gets closer to musical intention. On a Creative Journey tried increasingly detailed performance directions on one song and judged a later vocal smoother. The creator also allowed that repeated generations might explain the change, then cleaned stems and adjusted the vocal. That final mix cannot isolate the effect of the directions. In another demonstration, Produce Like a Boss’s Andrea Olsen supplied her voice memo and lyrics, asked Suno to keep her melody, and said it largely did, though the result still sounded artificial to her. | 3:24–4:10 | Would a listener care about those flaws? Caleb Porzio said on his podcast that he wanted to listen to a song made from his rough recording. He also described hearing artifacts in other AI covers he had enjoyed. His experience shows that enjoyment and noticing artifacts can coexist for one person; it does not predict another listener’s response. Return to our hypothetical chorus. The available tests offer no defensible overall v6 quality score or proof that a typical song is ready to release. Check your own export level, tonal balance, and response to directions, then make a listening decision about your own song.
