# Before you release a Suno-assisted song, map what went into it

*Evidence as of 2026-10-14 UTC.*

If you are turning your own demo into a release with Suno, the first useful question is specific: what did you write or perform, what did you upload, and what did Suno generate? A file-by-file account can help you explain your process. It cannot, by itself, establish ownership or tell you whether a distributor will accept the track. [03-c1] [03-c7]

The reporting and statements supplied for this article were published between September 21 and October 13, 2026. They give creators reasons to check their inputs, agreements and release plans. They do not supply the applicable Suno terms or a distributor’s decision on a documented Suno-assisted song. [03-c3] [03-c4] [03-c6] [03-c7]

## Keep a record you can understand later

On his *Is It Still Your Song?* podcast, practitioner Josh of JG Beats Lab recommends keeping the dated original demo, lyric sheet, settings, retained stems, raw vocal takes and final master together. He also suggests identifying each file’s origin: what you wrote, what Suno generated, and what you rebuilt from its output. That is practical workflow advice from a creator, not an ownership ruling. [03-c1]

The distinction matters because “made with Suno” can describe different kinds of work. In *Dazed*, artist Zemi says he makes the beats and toplines, sings, uses Suno to change his voice, and mixes the result himself. That is Zemi’s account; the quoted passage does not independently verify each production step. Still, it shows why a creator describing a song may need more detail than a single AI-assisted label. [03-c2]

Together, Josh’s method and Zemi’s account suggest a workable pre-release task: list the source of each contribution and preserve the files that show it. Before uploading a release candidate, use that record to identify exactly what will enter Suno and check the terms that apply to that use. This is an inference from their accounts and the regulator’s stated concern below, not a conclusion about rights in any particular track. [03-c7]

## Read the agreement as an agreement

The Italian Competition Authority announced on October 6 that it had opened an investigation focused on Suno’s Terms of Service. It said the scope of the license imposed on consumers as a condition of using the service *seems* insufficiently defined. That wording identifies a question under investigation; it is not a finding that Suno’s terms are unlawful. [03-c3]

For a creator, the immediate step is to check the applicable terms before uploading material, especially when the material includes contributions whose use you must be able to explain. The supplied evidence does not include the primary terms, their applicable version, or the circumstances of your track, so it cannot settle what rights you have or grant. Check the rules of each intended distributor or platform for the actual release, too: no specific distributor rule or decision is established here. [03-c7]

## Separate licensing statements from proven benefits

Commercial licensing news deserves the same care. The *Los Angeles Times* reports that musicians and songwriter groups are asking who can consent to AI licensing deals and who will be paid. According to the paper, the deals it discusses had not disclosed artist payouts, opt-in numbers or how much say individual musicians had over use of their work. Those missing details leave the benefit to an individual artist unverified. [03-c4]

*Music Business Worldwide* also reports Suno’s statement that training for v6 included licensed partner content and community interactions, including creations. In the same report, it describes allegations in a Sony and Universal complaint about training on earlier user-generated or altered audio, preference data, or both. Suno’s account is a company statement; the complaint’s account is an allegation, not a court finding. Neither establishes what permission or payment applies to your own song. [03-c5]

## Watch what happens after release

One platform development may become relevant to discovery. *Music Business Worldwide* reports Deezer’s plan for a search toggle that would let listeners exclude **fully AI-generated** music, with a rollout promised before the end of 2026. The supplied passage does not show that the toggle is live, explain its classification criteria, or say how it would treat a song with both human and Suno contributions. [03-c6]

Before release, make the contribution record, read the terms that apply to your upload, and ask each intended outlet which rules apply to your track. After release, watch for the Italian authority’s findings, clearer licensing disclosures, and Deezer’s actual rollout and classification rules. Each could answer a different open question; none of the current passages predicts the outcome for your song. [03-c3] [03-c4] [03-c6] [03-c7]
