# What Suno quality tests actually measure

*Evidence as of 2026-10-14 UTC.*

If you are trying to turn your own idea into a track you would release, “Is Suno v6 better?” is too broad a question to guide your next session. Recent tests offer narrower answers: one checks export levels, another compares spectra, and two creators describe what happened when they directed a song. Those results can help you decide what to test on your own material. They do not add up to an overall score for sound quality or creative control. [02-c7]

## A level measurement is not a listening verdict

In a September test, WaveDisco’s Serj regenerated three songs across v5.5 and three v6 variants, keeping the lyrics and prompt the same each time. He measured LUFS and True Peak with the same analysis pipeline. His conclusion was limited: the numbers changed, but he found no consistent direction of improvement on those measures. That is useful evidence about these outputs’ measured levels, rather than a finding that one version makes more compelling music. [02-c1]

Serj also compared WAV and MP3 exports where he had both. For two songs, he reported zero LUFS difference between formats across the three v6 variants. He called the two-song comparison an illustration and warned that other tracks may behave differently. If export loudness matters in your workflow, this gives you a reason to check your own files. It does not establish that every v6 export will match across formats. [02-c2]

Ytyng’s September analysis asks a different question. It reported that its v6 outputs had 2 to 3.4 dB less energy from 2 to 12 kHz and a 36% lower spectral centroid than its earlier tracks. A creator concerned about tonal balance may find that worth investigating. But ytyng acknowledged that the v6 and earlier songs came from different prompts and genres, and that the compared files used different codecs. It explicitly left open whether some or all of the measured difference came from the songs themselves. The result is a finding about that collection of files, not an isolated model effect. [02-c3]

Ytyng also cautioned that a song’s quality has little to do with its spectrum alone. That point matters when reading both tests: a level reading and a frequency comparison describe audio properties, while a decision to release a song involves judgments those measurements were not designed to make. [02-c7]

## Direction is a different kind of test

In an October video, On a Creative Journey tried adding performance directions to one song and preferred a later vocal result. The creator also allowed that repeated generations might account for the perceived improvement. The work then involved stem cleanup and vocal processing. A preferred final mix therefore cannot tell us how much of the change came from the directions, the choice among generations, or the later editing. It does show the sort of comparison a songwriter can make while working on a particular song. [02-c4]

Andrea Olsen of Produce Like a Boss described a related tension in another October video. After instructing Suno not to change a melody, she said it had kept the melody; she also said the result sounded like AI to her. That is her assessment of one attempt. It suggests a practical distinction between following a requested musical direction and satisfying the creator’s ear. It cannot supply a general rate at which Suno follows prompts. [02-c5]

A listener can hold similarly mixed views. On his October podcast, Caleb Porzio said he wanted to listen to a song made from his rough recording. Later, while discussing other AI covers he had enjoyed, he pointed out audible artifacts. His experience shows that enjoyment and artifact detection can coexist for one person; it does not predict whether another creator or listener would accept the same sound. [02-c6]

## Test the decision you actually face

The evidence supports a focused trial with your own song. Keep the input and settings consistent when comparing generations; inspect the exports if level or tonal balance matters; and listen separately for whether the melody, vocal delivery, and feel answer your direction. If you edit stems afterward, judge the generated take and the edited mix as separate stages. These are ways to evaluate the properties the cited tests examine, not a promise that any particular result will be ready to release. [02-c7]

What remains unknown is how v6 performs across a broad, matched set of creator-owned songs in a blinded listening test. The supplied tests use small or unmatched samples and different outcomes, and the accounts from creators and one podcast listener remain personal judgments. Independent mastering engineers and release listeners are also missing from this evidence. For now, the useful question is specific: *Does this output, after the work you are willing to do, meet your standard for this song?* [02-c7]
